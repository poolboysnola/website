Why it happened

Green isn't a mystery. It's arithmetic.

Algae is always in your pool. It blows in, rinses off swimmers, rides in on a pool float. None of that matters while there's enough free chlorine in the water to kill it faster than it can multiply. Green means that math stopped working — the chlorine ran out, or something stopped it from doing its job.

Usually it's both at once. A New Orleans August burns chlorine out of unstabilized water in a day, a thunderstorm dilutes what's left, and the heat has algae doubling overnight. Add a filter that hasn't been cleaned in a while and there's nothing left holding the line.

Which is why "just dump in some shock" so often fails. If the pH is high, most of that chlorine is sitting in the water as the weak stuff and barely sanitizing; if the filter isn't pulling its weight, dead algae stays suspended and the water goes from green to cloudy grey and stalls there. Clearing a pool for good means fixing the reason, not just the color.

The usual suspects

Chlorine bottomed out
The most common one by a mile. No stabilizer in the water and the sun strips it out in hours.
pH drifted up
High pH doesn't just sting eyes — it makes the chlorine you already paid for far less effective.
The filter quit working
Clogged, channelled, or only running four hours a day. Water that doesn't circulate is water algae likes.
Nobody brushed
Algae grabs the shaded walls, steps, and tanning ledge first. Brushing is what breaks it loose.
Rain, heat, or a storm
Debris in, chemistry diluted, temperature up. A green pool a week after a storm is normal, not bad luck.
Why it matters

It's not just ugly.

Green water is the visible part. What's actually wrong is that nothing in the pool is killing anything anymore.

Bacteria, not just algae

Algae itself mostly won't hurt you. The problem is that unsanitized water lets E. coli, pseudomonas, and everything else ride along with it. Green is the signal that your chlorine stopped working days ago.

Itchy skin, red eyes, ear infections

Wildly off pH and no sanitizer is a recipe for irritation, rashes, and swimmer's ear. If a swim leaves everyone scratching, that's the water talking, not the chlorine.

A mosquito nursery

This is New Orleans. Still, untreated water in a backyard is exactly where mosquitoes want to lay eggs, and a neglected pool can supply an entire block.

How we do it

Six steps, in this order, for a reason.

Every one of these gets undone by skipping the one before it. That's the whole trick.

01

Test it and find the cause

Full panel — pH, free and combined chlorine, alkalinity, stabilizer, hardness, phosphates if we suspect them. We also look at the pump, the filter, and how long it actually runs. No point treating a pool that's going to go green again in three weeks.

02

Get the debris out

Leaves, live-oak litter, branches, and whatever the last storm contributed. Organic debris eats chlorine, so pulling it out first means the shock goes toward algae instead of a pile of wet leaves.

03

Shock it properly

pH gets corrected first so the chlorine is actually in its effective form, then we dose to break-point — enough to finish the bloom in one go. Half a dose twice is worse than one done right, and it costs you more.

04

Brush every surface

Walls, steps, ledges, behind the ladder, the corners the robot ignores. Algae builds a film that shrugs off chlorine until it's physically broken up — this is the step that separates a clear pool from one that greens back up next week.

05

Make the filter earn its keep

Clean or backwash, then run it long — often around the clock for a few days. Killing algae only turns it into dead algae, and dead algae is what makes water cloudy. The filter is what gets it out.

06

Rebalance and hand it back

Alkalinity, then pH, then stabilizer so the sun doesn't undo all of it, plus calcium hardness so the water stops looking for minerals in your plaster. You get a written note with where every number landed.

Before you book

The questions we get most.

How bad is too bad?

Nearly nothing is too far gone. We've cleared pools you couldn't see six inches into. The honest exception is a pool that's been standing for months with mud, silt, and a dead-leaf layer on the bottom — sometimes draining and cleaning it beats treating it. We'll look at yours and tell you which is cheaper.

Do you have to drain my pool?

Almost never, and we won't suggest it lightly. Draining a pool in this ground is its own risk — high water tables around here can float a shell or crack plaster that's been left dry in the sun. Treating the water is usually faster, cheaper, and safer.

How much does a recovery cost?

It depends on the size of the pool and how far gone it is, so we quote it after we've seen it — not from a photo and a guess. What you'll get is one price for the recovery, including chemicals and the return visits it takes to finish the job.

Can I swim during the recovery?

No. Chlorine sits well above normal for the first few days, and you shouldn't be swimming in water you can't see through anyway. We'll tell you the day it's safe, based on a test rather than a hunch.

Should I go weekly afterward?

We'd say yes, and not just because we sell it. A freshly recovered pool is a pool whose chemistry was recently off enough to bloom — holding it steady for the first month or two is when it matters most. Weekly or bi-weekly service handles it.

Probably — we run routes from Metairie to the Bywater and up over the lake. Check the service area, and if you're just outside the lines, ask anyway. We make exceptions.
